Standard 1 - High Expectations

Set high expectations which inspire, motivate, challenge pupils.
Evidence 1 (Observations/Reflections)
DLD Cycle 22 (Computing Revision). Identified the need to establish consequences early rather than relying solely on rapport. This ensured high behavioral expectations were met and maintained lesson pace.
Evidence 2 (Planning/Assessment)
DLD Cycle 19 (MakeCode Arcade). Designed "hard mode" and multiple coding paths (e.g., monster truck coding) to push higher prior attainers, preventing "more of the same" busywork.
Evidence 3 (Professional Engagement)
DLD Cycle 20 (Cyber Security). Challenged Year 9 pupils to justify their decisions in cyber scenarios rather than passively accepting answers. Prompted them to think critically about "black hat" vs "white hat" roles.
💡

Pedagogy in Practice

I've learned that high expectations aren't just about behavior, but about cognitive challenge. Drawing on the Pygmalion Effect (Rosenthal & Jacobson, 1968) - which shows that higher expectations lead to an increase in performance - I provided multiple coding paths in MakeCode to keep high attainers stretched. Simultaneously, I learned to lean on the school's consequence system early to maintain a safe, predictable learning environment.

Standard 2 - Promote Good Progress

Promote good progress and outcomes by pupils.
Evidence 1 (Classroom Practice)
DLD Cycle 18 (Graphite). Shifted from direct instruction to structured 'Teach-Check-Practice' cycles, allowing students to check their vector designs iteratively.
Evidence 2 (Subject Knowledge/Planning)
DLD Cycle 23 (Pathfinding Algorithms). Moved away from front-loaded theory to interactive checking tools for Year 12s, which actively progressed the lesson based on their grasp of the heuristic learning pit.
Evidence 3 (Assessment Data)
DLD Cycle 21 (Classfolio Retrieval). Used live Classfolio retrieval data to consolidate Year 8 knowledge across the term, ensuring gaps were closed before moving forward.
Evidence 4 (Live Assessment & Moderation)
Classfolio Results. What the data shows: 26 assessment runs across 20 classes, auto-marked then moderated and reviewed (Years 7 to 10), including a Year 8 end-of-topic with 29 pupils and 522 responses. Teaching decision: I moderate the automated marks before release and compare classes (e.g. 8A2 against 8b3) to pinpoint where a cohort needs reteaching.
Evidence 5 (Live Pupil Folios)
Student Folios dashboard. What the data shows: 9 pupil folios holding 123 evidence items across 32 classes, each one building automatically from pupil input and flagged when it needs review. Teaching decision: I work through flagged folios and write feedback and targets straight into each pupil's book.
Evidence 6 (Per-Pupil Data & Targets)
Individual folio (9B4, Cyber Security). What the data shows: a Drag and Drop task auto-marked 4/4, part of a folio built from 5 lessons and 18 responses. Teaching decision: off the back of the data I set this pupil's targets, "Complete revision task 3" and "Explain your reasoning in more detail".
💡

Pedagogy in Practice

Progress relies on checking understanding constantly. Influenced by Roediger & Karpicke's (2006) research on the 'testing effect' and retrieval practice, I used Teach-Check-Practice cycles in Graphite and leveraged Classfolio for live retrieval. This meant I could adapt the pace of the lesson based on actual data rather than assumptions. I also marked nine Year 12 mock papers, moderating my judgements against the mark scheme with the class teacher to build an accurate picture of where pupils were and what to reteach.

Standard 4 - Classroom Practice

Plan and teach well structured lessons.
Evidence 1 (Classroom Practice)
DLD Cycle 21. Seamlessly integrated the Classfolio platform into lessons, creating structured, fast-paced transitions between multiple short retrieval activities.
Evidence 2 (Observations)
DLD Cycle 19. Transitioned to a structured "I do, We do, You do" modelling approach when teaching new coding concepts, significantly reducing cognitive overload.
Evidence 3 (Reflections)
DLD Cycles 10 & 11. Used planned hinge questions to script vocabulary and pivot lessons based on real-time understanding.
💡

Pedagogy in Practice

Structure is everything. Guided by Rosenshine's Principles of Instruction (2012), specifically around sequencing concepts and modelling, I moved from assuming knowledge to explicit 'I do, We do, You do' modelling. Deploying platforms like Classfolio allowed me to structure transitions tightly and use hinge questions to pivot when needed.

Standard 5 - Adapt Teaching

Adapt teaching to respond to the strengths and needs of all pupils.
Evidence 1 (SEND Support)
DLD Cycle 20. Implemented "mini-wedo" physical keyboard scaffolding (guiding hands on the keyboard) for SEND pupils struggling with typing literacy.
Evidence 2 (Differentiation)
DLD Cycle 18. Reflected on the differences between A-band and B-band groups, realizing B-band required more staggered "We do" handovers and smaller chunked steps for vector design.
Evidence 3 (EAL Support)
DLD Cycle 19. Adapted delivery by reading instructions out loud and summarizing visually for EAL pupils, lowering the barrier to entry for coding tasks.
Evidence 4 (Whole-Class Inclusive Planning)
Inclusive Planning Grid (all classes). What it shows: a per-class grid mapping each pupil's area of need, across SEND (dyslexia, dyscalculia, dyspraxia, dysgraphia, Down Syndrome) and physical or sensory needs, to specific strategies, with those effective for EAL learners flagged. Teaching decision: adaptation is planned in advance per class and tied to the seating plan, so SEND and EAL needs are met systematically rather than reactively.
💡

Pedagogy in Practice

A 'one size fits all' approach doesn't work. Applying Sweller's (1988) Cognitive Load Theory, I adapted my Graphite lesson entirely for a B-band group by staggering the handovers to reduce intrinsic load. For SEND pupils lacking typing literacy, I used physical 'mini-wedo' scaffolding to build their confidence without overloading their working memory.

Standard 6 - Assessment

Make accurate and productive use of assessment.
Evidence 1 (Live AfL)
DLD Cycle 22. Used live Classfolio assessment data to enforce an 80% class accuracy threshold before allowing the lesson to proceed. What the data shows: after targeted reteaching of PSD vs JPEG, class knowledge rose toward the 80% gate - the screenshot is the decision point, not just a dashboard.
Evidence 2 (Questioning)
DLD Cycle 17. Reflected on the danger of asking "Is everyone ok with this?" and shifted to targeted, probing questions on network terminology to expose hidden misconceptions.
Evidence 3 (Formative Strategies)
Course Development Log. Integrated gamified formative assessments like "spot the CARP" and "match the tool" into plenaries to assess KS3 design principles effectively.
💡

Pedagogy in Practice

I learned early on that asking 'Does everyone understand?' is a trap. Grounding my approach in Wiliam & Black's (1998) 'Inside the Black Box' on formative assessment, I replaced this with targeted questioning and strict 80% accuracy thresholds on Classfolio before moving on.

Standard 7 - Managing Behaviour

Manage behaviour effectively to ensure a good and safe learning environment.
Evidence 1 (Reflections)
DLD Cycle 22. Addressed personal bias regarding positive rapport; committed to using the consequence system earlier to prevent behavior from slipping during independent tasks.
Evidence 2 (Routines)
DLD Cycle 19. Implemented strict visual timers and cut-off points to ensure transitions were crisp and didn't bleed into off-task chatting.
Evidence 3 (Classroom Management)
DLD Cycle 12. Actively managed student behavior during plenaries by ensuring the "turn and face the board" routine was established and enforced before speaking.
💡

Pedagogy in Practice

A safe environment requires consistency. Reflecting on Bill Rogers' behavior management strategies regarding the establishment of routines and positive correction, I realized I couldn't rely purely on my rapport with students - I needed to use the school's consequence system early and stick to strict timers to maintain a focused learning environment.
